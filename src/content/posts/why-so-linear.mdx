This isn't an accident: linearity is computationally tractable, mathematically elegant, and deeply wired into our hardware. But biology is nonlinear, and the gap between our linear tools and nonlinear reality has consequences. The missing heritability problem in genomics, the limitations of GWAS and TWAS, and the inability of associational methods to produce causal claims all trace back to our dependence on linearity. This post explores why we default to lines, what that costs us in applied genetics and data science, and how causal inference frameworks offer a path beyond association."

# The Mathematics of "Close Enough": Linearization and Taylor's Theorem

{/* [TODO] Maybe good to add a D3 animation of this for a visual cue??
*/}

The why, (un)fortunately begets *some* calculus.
The [fundamental theorem of calculus]() and [Taylor series]() give us mathematical license to approximate any smooth function as a line (or polynomial) near a point.
This is perhaps the deepest reason linearity is everywhere -- it's the first order approximation that *works* locally.
Taylor's theorem: `f(x) ≈ f(a) + f'(a)(x - a) for x near a`

When we expand to look at sytems of [differential equations]() we treat these systems in a similar linear fashion around equilibria (Jacobian matrices).
The entire foundation of differential calculus rests on the idea that smooth functions are "locally linear".

TLDR: Calculus is often the art of zooming in until curves look like lines.

# Why Computers Love Lines: Hardware, Bits, and Linear Algebra

Current classical bit driven compute is fundamentally optimized to linear algebra operations.
Classical [bits]() are binary which arithmetically reduces to linear algebra over finite fields.
Matrix multiplication is the atomic operation of modern computing paradigms (deep learning, graphics, simulation, fluid dynamics).
Deep learning is also an interesting case in that it relies on a [manifold]() distilling the [tangent space]() into a [Jacobian matrix]().
These operations are so fundamental to computers, that the [FORTRAN]() libraries [BLAS]() and [LAPACK]() underpin everything from numpy to TensorFlow.
Consider the physical geometry of NVIDIA's CUDA cores-- it is entirely designed around linear algebra operations.
I've always quipped that deep learning is a large number of linear algebra operations in a trenchcoat.
Floating point linear operations are numerically stable, while nonlinear operations tend to compound rounding errors.

Classical metabolic modeling relies heavily on [convexity optimization]() applied to matrices.
By guaranteeing global optima through linear and convex optimization, you can estimate the metabolic state of an organism from the data gathered.
If you delve deep enough into metabolic modeling you start to see that commercial solvers are effectively FORTRAN code for hire--and yet more linear algebra operations.
Now, if we were to apply nonlinear optimization we can find local minima but there are no guarantees that the search doesn't drag on toward infinity.
I think metabolic modeling is one of the fields I'm more excited about people applying quantum compute to it.
Qubits inherently represent nonlinear state spaces while in superposition.
However, even some quantum algorithms use linear algebra--just in an exponentially larger vector space

# The Statistics of Linearity: Why OLS Rules the World

{/* [TODO] Explain why linear models dominate statistics, from
    Gauss through modern machine learning.

    Key ideas:
    - Gauss-Markov theorem: among all linear unbiased estimators,
      OLS has the minimum variance (BLUE). This is a *mathematical
      guarantee* — you literally cannot do better within the class
      of linear unbiased estimators
    - Generalized linear models (GLMs) extend linearity through
      link functions — logistic regression, Poisson regression, etc.
      are "linear" in the sense that the link-transformed mean is
      a linear function of predictors
    - Linear mixed models: random effects are additive/linear
    - Interpretability: coefficients in linear models have direct
      interpretations ("a one-unit increase in X is associated with
      a β-unit change in Y"). Nonlinear models sacrifice this
    - Identifiability: linear model parameters are uniquely
      determined by the data (given sufficient rank). Nonlinear
      models often have multiple parameter configurations that
      fit equally well
    - Convergence: linear model fitting is a single matrix
      inversion (or SVD). No iterative optimization, no
      hyperparameter tuning, no convergence failure
    - The Shmueli distinction: prediction vs. explanation. Linear
      models are often chosen for *explanation*, even when nonlinear
      models predict better

    For biologists: "When your statistician collaborator runs a
    'regression,' they're fitting a line. When they run a 'mixed
    model,' they're fitting a line with random wobble. When they
    run 'logistic regression,' they're fitting a line on a
    transformed scale. It's lines all the way down."
*/}

# Fisher's Infinite Line: The Additive Model in Quantitative Genetics

{/* [TODO] This is where you connect the mathematical/computational
    story to biology. Fisher's 1918 infinitesimal model is the
    founding assumption of quantitative genetics — and it's linear.

    Key ideas:
    - Fisher (1918): phenotype = sum of infinitely many small
      additive genetic effects + environment. This is a *linear*
      decomposition
    - The infinitesimal model assumes traits are the sum of
      contributions from many loci, each of small effect, with
      additive (i.e., linear) contributions
    - Barton, Etheridge & Véber (2017) rigorously derived the
      infinitesimal model as the limit of Mendelian inheritance
      with many loci — the additivity emerges mathematically as
      M → ∞
    - This additive assumption is baked into GWAS: the standard
      GWAS model tests each SNP for a linear (additive) effect on
      phenotype, one at a time
    - Heritability (h²) is defined as the proportion of phenotypic
      variance attributable to *additive* genetic variance — it's
      a linear quantity by definition
    - The entire breeding value concept (used in animal/plant
      breeding) is a linear predictor

    The punchline: "We didn't choose linear models for genetics
    because biology is linear. We chose them because Fisher showed
    that under certain conditions, the *additive* component is what
    selection acts on — and because linear models were the only
    ones we could compute in 1918."
*/}

**References to read:**
- R.A. Fisher (1918), ["The Correlation Between Relatives on the Supposition of Mendelian Inheritance"](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0080456800012163), *Transactions of the Royal Society of Edinburgh* — the founding paper of quantitative genetics
- Barton, Etheridge & Véber (2017), ["The infinitesimal model: Definition, derivation, and implications"](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tpb.2017.06.001), *Theoretical Population Biology* — rigorous modern treatment
- Hill & Barton (2019), ["From R.A. Fisher's 1918 Paper to GWAS a Century Later"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6456325/), *Genetics* — connects the infinitesimal model directly to modern GWAS methodology
- Lynch & Walsh, *Genetics and Analysis of Quantitative Traits* (1998) — the standard textbook; heavily linear algebra throughout

# GWAS and TWAS: Linear Tools for a Nonlinear Genome

{/* [TODO] Now the critique. GWAS and TWAS are fundamentally
    linear methods, and this has consequences.

    GWAS:
    - Standard model: y = Xβ + Zu + e, where β are fixed SNP
      effects (tested one at a time) and u is a random polygenic
      effect (linear mixed model)
    - Tests for *marginal* additive effects — misses epistasis,
      dominance, gene-gene interactions
    - The "missing heritability" problem: GWAS hits explain only
      a fraction of estimated heritability. Where's the rest?
    - Manolio et al. (2009): coined "missing heritability" — GWAS
      variants explain ~5-10% of variance for most complex traits
      despite high heritability estimates
    - Zuk et al. (2012): "phantom heritability" — epistatic
      interactions inflate heritability estimates, making the
      "missing" portion partly an artifact of the additive
      assumption itself

    TWAS:
    - Two-stage approach: (1) train a linear model predicting gene
      expression from genotype, (2) test association between
      predicted expression and trait
    - Both stages are linear — Gusev et al. (2016) used elastic
      net (linear penalty) and BLUP (linear mixed model)
    - TWAS inherits all GWAS limitations plus adds the assumption
      that genotype → expression → trait is a *linear chain*
    - Cannot distinguish causation from pleiotropy or linkage
      without additional tests (HEIDI, colocalization)

    What's missing:
    - Epistasis (gene-gene interactions)
    - Gene-environment interactions
    - Nonlinear dose-response relationships
    - Network effects / pathway-level biology
    - Causal direction (is gene expression causing the trait,
      or does the trait affect expression?)
*/}

**References to read:**
- Manolio et al. (2009), ["Finding the missing heritability of complex diseases"](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature08494), *Nature* — the landmark missing heritability paper
- Zuk, Hechter, Sunyaev, Lander (2012), ["The mystery of missing heritability: Genetic interactions create phantom heritability"](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1119675109), *PNAS* — epistasis inflates heritability estimates
- Gusev et al. (2016), ["Integrative approaches for large-scale transcriptome-wide association studies"](https://doi.org/10.1038/ng.3506), *Nature Genetics* — the original TWAS/FUSION paper
- Zhu et al. (2016), ["Integration of summary data from GWAS and eQTL studies predicts complex trait gene targets"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27019110/), *Nature Genetics* — SMR/HEIDI method
- Wainberg et al. (2019), ["Opportunities and challenges for transcriptome-wide association studies"](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-019-0385-z), *Nature Genetics* — TWAS limitations review
- Yang et al. (2010), ["Common SNPs explain a large proportion of the heritability for human height"](https://www.nature.com/articles/ng.608), *Nature Genetics* — GCTA showing most heritability is captured by common SNPs collectively, just not individually significant
- Palmer et al. (2023), ["Discovering non-additive heritability using additive GWAS summary statistics"](https://elifesciences.org/articles/90459), *eLife* — evidence of pairwise epistasis across 25/25 UK Biobank traits

# Beyond Lines: Toward Causal Models

{/* [TODO] This is where you pivot from critique to aspiration.
    The desire for causal output, not just association.

    Key ideas:
    - Association ≠ causation, but our entire GWAS/TWAS framework
      produces *associations*. The Manhattan plot is a map of
      correlation, not causation
    - Judea Pearl's causal hierarchy: (1) seeing/association,
      (2) doing/intervention, (3) imagining/counterfactual.
      GWAS operates at level 1. We want level 2 or 3.
    - Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs): make causal assumptions
      explicit. A DAG for GWAS would show genotype → expression →
      trait, but also confounders, reverse causation, pleiotropy
    - Mendelian randomization (MR): uses genetic variants as
      "instrumental variables" to estimate causal effects. It's
      still fundamentally linear (IV regression), but it gives
      you a causal *direction*
    - Colocalization (Giambartolomei et al. 2014): Bayesian test
      for whether two traits share a causal variant — helps
      distinguish pleiotropy from linkage
    - Structural equation models (SEMs): can encode nonlinear
      causal relationships, but require strong assumptions
    - Kernel methods: "linear in a transformed space" — the
      kernel trick lets you fit nonlinear decision boundaries
      using linear algebra in a higher-dimensional feature space.
      This is a clever middle ground
    - Deep learning for genomics: can capture nonlinear
      interactions but sacrifices interpretability and often
      requires enormous datasets
    - Visible Neural Networks (VNNs) use biological pathway
      structure to define sparse, interpretable architectures —
      a promising middle ground (Huang et al. 2025)
    - BUT: a sobering 2025 Nature Comms result (Kerin et al.)
      found that neural networks "detect limited nonlinearity
      and do not outperform linear models" for polygenic score
      prediction — for many traits, the additive signal dominates
    - The tension: as we move from linear to nonlinear to causal,
      we gain realism but lose tractability, interpretability,
      and statistical power

    The honest assessment: "We don't yet have a method that gives
    us nonlinear causal genomics at scale. But understanding *why*
    we've been stuck on lines is the first step toward getting
    past them."
*/}

**References to read:**
- Judea Pearl, [*The Book of Why*](https://www.basicbooks.com/titles/judea-pearl/the-book-of-why/9780465097616/) (2018) — accessible introduction to causal inference for a general audience
- Pearl (2009), [*Causality: Models, Reasoning, and Inference*](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511803161), 2nd ed. — the technical treatment of do-calculus and structural causal models

- Morrison et al. (2020), ["Mendelian randomization accounting for correlated and uncorrelated pleiotropic effects using genome-wide summary statistics"](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-020-0631-4), *Nature Genetics* — the CAUSE method

# Conclusion

{/* [TODO] Tie it together.

    - Linearity isn't a failure of imagination — it's a rational
      response to computational, mathematical, and statistical
      constraints
    - But mistaking the map for the territory is dangerous:
      biology doesn't care about our computational convenience
    - The path forward isn't abandoning linear methods — it's
      knowing when they're appropriate and when they're hiding
      the biology
    - For GWAS/TWAS specifically: we need methods that can
      (a) capture nonlinear genetic architecture,
      (b) provide causal direction, and
      (c) remain computationally tractable at biobank scale
    - That's a tall order. But being honest about the limitations
      of our current tools is how science advances.

    End with something that connects back to both audiences:
    biologists who want actionable mechanisms, and data scientists
    who want principled methodology.
